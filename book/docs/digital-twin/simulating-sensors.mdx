# Simulating Sensors: LiDAR, Depth Cameras, IMU

[Chapter 2.2](./physics-gravity-collisions.mdx) ended on a specific complaint. The humanoid has
0.30 seconds and 4.8 degrees of margin before it tips, and nothing in the model can tell it which
way it is leaning. It is a body with no way to find out anything.

This chapter fixes the finding-out. Three sensors go on the robot — a LiDAR on the chest, a depth
camera in the head, an IMU in the pelvis — and one Python node reads all three. That is the whole
job, and it is smaller than it sounds: a simulated sensor is a query the simulator runs against the
scene on a fixed schedule, and the answer is a ROS 2 message of a type you can look up.

The part that is *not* small is knowing what each answer means, and where it is a lie.

One requirement goes beyond the rest of this module's, so it is stated here rather than buried
later: the LiDAR and the depth camera are *rendered*, through OGRE 2, and so they need a machine
with working GPU rendering. The IMU and all of the physics do not. A reader without that machine can
follow every idea in this chapter and run the IMU half of the example; the fuller version of this
note is in [Environment](#environment) below.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Add a LiDAR, a depth camera, and an IMU to this book's humanoid, and say which link each is
  attached to and where on that link it sits.
- Name the ROS 2 message type and the frame each sensor publishes in, and find both from the
  command line when you do not know them.
- Read all three sensors from a single Python node and extract one meaningful number from each.
- Explain what sensor noise models and why developing against a noiseless sensor produces code that
  fails on hardware.

## Why simulate a sensor at all

[Chapter 2.1](./gazebo-setup-and-basics.mdx) gave three reasons to simulate anything: mistakes are
cheap, hardware is slow to arrive, and initial conditions repeat. All three sharpen when the subject
is perception.

**The hardware argument is strongest here.** A perception stack is the longest-lead software on a
robot, and it is the software most tightly coupled to parts that have not been bought yet. A team
that waits for a LiDAR before writing anything that consumes LiDAR has serialised two long jobs that
could have run in parallel. A simulated sensor publishing the right message type on the right topic
lets the consumer be written, tested, and largely debugged first.

**The repeatability argument is the one you will use daily.** Perception bugs are usually about a
specific input: the moment the wall left the field of view, the frame where every return was
`inf`. On hardware, reproducing that means recreating a scene. In simulation the same scene replays
exactly, so a fix can be *demonstrated* rather than hoped for.

**The ground-truth argument is new.** Only in simulation do you know the real answer. The wall in
this chapter's world is at *x* = 3.0 m because you put it there, so when the LiDAR says 2.90 m you
can check that number rather than believe it. That is the single largest advantage simulated
perception has, and it disappears the moment the robot is real.

Now the other side, stated plainly, because it is where simulated perception misleads.

**A simulated sensor sees the simulated world, and the simulated world is thin.** Gazebo's ground
plane has no puddles, the wall has no glass panel, and there is no dust, rain, or direct sunlight in
the scene. Every one of those is a first-order failure mode for a real LiDAR or depth camera.

**Rendering-based sensors carry rendering's assumptions.** A depth camera in Gazebo asks the
renderer how far away each pixel is. The renderer knows exactly; a real depth camera is
triangulating or timing light, and is defeated by black, shiny, or transparent surfaces that the
renderer handles without noticing.

**The noise model is a caricature.** You will add Gaussian noise below, and Gaussian noise is a
reasonable first approximation of a LiDAR's ranging error. It is not a good model of a depth
camera's error, which grows roughly with the *square* of distance and arrives in structured patches
rather than independently per pixel. Gazebo's depth camera does not model that at all, which makes
it the most flattering of the three sensors in this chapter and the one most likely to embarrass
you later.

The honest summary: simulated sensors are excellent for the *shape* of your data — types, frames,
rates, coordinate conventions, the plumbing — and unreliable for its *quality*.

## What a sensor is, inside the simulator

Every sensor in Gazebo Harmonic is the same three things.

1. **An attachment.** The sensor belongs to a link, and inherits that link's motion. Its own `<pose>`
   offsets it from the link's frame.
2. **A schedule.** `<update_rate>` in hertz. The sensor is evaluated on simulation steps, so its rate
   is in *simulated* time — a sensor at 100 Hz produces 100 readings per simulated second whether
   that takes one wall-clock second or ten. This is the same two-clocks problem
   [Chapter 2.1](./gazebo-setup-and-basics.mdx) described, and the reason every node here runs with
   `use_sim_time: true`.
3. **A query, plus noise, plus a message.** On each scheduled tick the simulator asks the scene a
   question, corrupts the answer according to the sensor's noise model, and publishes it on a Gazebo
   Transport topic.

The queries differ, and that is the interesting part.

### LiDAR: ray casting

A LiDAR measures distance by sending out light and timing its return. The simulator does not
simulate light. It **casts rays**: for each beam, it takes a start point and a direction, walks
outward through the scene's collision geometry, and reports the distance to the first surface hit —
or infinity if nothing is hit inside the maximum range.

Three consequences follow immediately, and all three surprise people.

**It sees `<visual>` geometry, not `<collision>` geometry** — and this is the exact opposite of what
[Chapter 2.2](./physics-gravity-collisions.mdx) established about contact. The sensor type is
`gpu_lidar` because the ray casting runs on the graphics hardware, against the scene the *renderer*
holds. Chapter 1.4's head makes
the consequence concrete: physics sees a 0.12 m sphere and no visor; a LiDAR pointed at this
humanoid sees a 0.11 m skull *and* the orange visor box. Two sensors, in the same simulator, looking
at two different heads. Whenever a robot appears to detect something it does not collide with — or
walks straight through something its LiDAR could see — this is why.

**Cost is per ray.** A scan of 360 beams costs 360 ray casts every tick, so beams × update rate is
the number to watch.

**A beam that hits nothing returns infinity**, not zero and not the maximum range. Code that treats
`ranges` as a list of numbers will compute a mean of `inf` on its first frame in an open field. The
`sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan` message carries `range_min` and `range_max` so consumers can discard
what falls outside them. Gazebo happens to clamp every *finite* range into that interval before
publishing, so against this simulator the bounds check never actually fires — but a real driver
makes no such promise, and the check is one line.

**A `gpu_lidar` publishes two topics, not one.** Alongside the `LaserScan`, `gz-sensors` advertises
the same returns as a point cloud on `<topic>/points`. This chapter's node ignores it; the topic
list below will not.

### Depth cameras: per-pixel distance

A depth camera produces an image whose pixel values are **distances in metres** rather than colours.
Gazebo renders the scene from the camera's viewpoint and reads back the depth of each pixel, which
is information the renderer computes anyway in order to decide which surface is in front.

So a depth camera is, roughly, a very dense LiDAR confined to a rectangle. The differences that
matter:

- **Resolution and field of view are pixels and an angle**, not beams and a sweep. A 320 × 240 depth
  image is 76,800 distances per frame.
- **The data type is float32, one channel.** Bridged into ROS 2 it arrives as a
  `sensor_msgs/msg/Image` with `encoding` `32FC1` — four bytes per pixel, metres. It is not an
  8-bit picture, and viewing it as one shows you noise.
- **Nothing beyond `<clip><far>` exists.** Pixels past the far clip plane come back non-finite, the
  same "no return" case as the LiDAR's infinity, arriving through a different door.

The sensor also publishes a point cloud (`sensor_msgs/msg/PointCloud2`) on `<topic>/points` and
intrinsics (`sensor_msgs/msg/CameraInfo`) on a **sibling** of `<topic>` — not a child of it. That
one is worth knowing before you go looking for it. `gz-sensors` builds the info topic by splitting
the sensor's topic on `/`, **dropping the last segment**, and appending `/camera_info`, so a sensor
with `<topic>humanoid/depth</topic>` publishes its intrinsics on `/humanoid/camera_info`. (SDFormat
1.11 does define a `<camera_info_topic>` element to override this, but Gazebo Harmonic's
`DepthCameraSensor` never reads it — only the plain `camera` sensor does. For a depth camera the
sibling rule is the whole story.) Both extra topics are Module 3's business; the chapter's node
reads the depth image.

### IMU: what is actually being measured

An **inertial measurement unit** is two instruments in one package: a three-axis accelerometer and a
three-axis gyroscope. The gyroscope is straightforward — angular velocity about each body axis, in
rad/s. The accelerometer is not, and misreading it is the most common sensor mistake in robotics.

**An accelerometer does not measure acceleration. It measures *proper* acceleration** — the
acceleration of the sensor relative to free fall. The classic demonstration is a sensor sitting
still on a table: it is not accelerating at all, and it reads 9.8 m/s² *upward*. Drop it, and while
it falls at 9.8 m/s² it reads **zero**.

That inversion is what makes the IMU useful for the problem Chapter 2.2 left open. A stationary IMU
reads a vector of magnitude 9.8 m/s² pointing along the sensor's local "up", so the direction of
that vector tells you which way is down — which is a tilt measurement, from an instrument with no
view of the outside world. It is also why an IMU alone cannot tell you where the robot *is*: getting
position out of acceleration takes two integrations, and two integrations of a slightly biased
signal drift away from the truth quadratically. Fixing that means combining the IMU with something
else, which is sensor fusion, which is Module 3's job and not this chapter's.

The bridged message is `sensor_msgs/msg/Imu`, carrying `linear_acceleration`, `angular_velocity`,
and an `orientation` quaternion. Be careful with that third field: on a real IMU orientation is a
*derived* estimate, not a measurement, and Gazebo fills it from the simulator's ground truth. Trust
it in simulation and your code will have nothing to fall back on when the hardware arrives.

## Where a sensor sits: frames

Every reading is a set of numbers relative to something. That something is a **frame**, and getting
it wrong is not a small error — it is an answer to a different question.

[Chapter 1.4](../ros2/urdf-describing-a-humanoid.mdx) already gave you the machinery: every link's
frame is defined by the joint above it, and a `fixed` joint "moves nothing but adds a frame — what a
camera or an IMU reports its data in". The three sensors here take the shorter route and attach
directly to existing links with a `<pose>` offset, which changes not one mass or inertia value in
Chapter 2.2's analysis. (A real project usually does give each sensor its own link on a `fixed`
joint. Note before you try it that libsdformat's URDF-to-SDF converter merges fixed-joint links into
their parent by default, so the frame you carefully named may not survive the conversion.)

The placements below are chosen, not arbitrary:

| Sensor | Link | Why there |
|---|---|---|
| IMU | `base_link` (pelvis) | Near the centre of mass, so it measures the *body's* motion rather than a swinging limb's. |
| LiDAR | `torso` | Chest height, forward-facing, above the legs. |
| Depth camera | `head` | Behind the visor, so it looks where the head looks — and turns when `neck_joint` turns. |

That last row is the whole lesson. The depth camera is on the head, so its readings are in the head's
frame, so turning the neck changes what "2.87 metres straight ahead" means without changing a single
number in the camera's configuration. Every reading in this chapter is a distance *from a place on a
moving body*, and the wall at *x* = 3.0 m is 2.90 m from the chest LiDAR and 2.87 m from the head
camera for no reason other than that the two sensors are 3 cm apart in *x*.

In ROS 2 the frame travels with the data, in `header.frame_id`. The chapter's node prints that field
the first time each sensor reports, so you read it off the wire instead of assuming it.

## Noise, and the habit a clean sensor teaches

Turn noise off and every example in this chapter still works. That is exactly the danger.

Real sensors are wrong in characteristic ways, and the SDF noise model names three of them:

- **`stddev`** — random scatter around the true value, different every reading.
- **`bias_mean`** and **`bias_stddev`** — a constant offset drawn once when the sensor starts and
  held for its lifetime. Averaging does not remove it. This is what makes a still IMU report a slow
  rotation forever.
- **`dynamic_bias_stddev`** with **`dynamic_bias_correlation_time`** — a bias that wanders slowly.
  The reason an IMU that was accurate at the start of a run is not at the end.

Now the habits a noiseless sensor teaches, all of which look like clean code at the time:

**Equality and thresholds that only just work.** `if distance < 1.0` is fine when the reading is
exactly 1.02. With 1 cm of scatter it flickers between branches ten times a second. Every real
threshold needs hysteresis, and you will not discover that against a clean sensor.

**Trusting a single reading.** One clean sample is the truth, so you write code that acts on one
sample. One noisy sample is a vote, so you write code that filters — and the difference in structure
is large enough that it is not a change you make later.

**Never handling the missing case.** A perfect sensor in a small world always returns something. Add
noise and an open direction, and `inf` and `NaN` become routine.

The rule that follows: **develop against a noisy sensor and turn the noise off to debug**, never the
other way round. The numbers below have `stddev` values in the range real devices report — 1 cm for
the LiDAR, and IMU figures typical of the kind of small solid-state part a robot this size would
carry.

## From a Gazebo topic to a ROS 2 topic

Nothing new here, which is the point:
[Chapter 2.1](./gazebo-setup-and-basics.mdx) established that Gazebo Transport and DDS are separate
systems and that `ros_gz_bridge` translates named topics one at a time, in a direction you choose
with `[`, `]`, or `@`. Sensors change none of that; they only add topics to bridge.

The pairings you need, from the
[`ros_gz_bridge` mapping table](https://github.com/gazebosim/ros_gz/blob/jazzy/ros_gz_bridge/README.md):

| Sensor | SDF `type` | Gazebo message | ROS 2 message |
|---|---|---|---|
| LiDAR | `gpu_lidar` | `gz.msgs.LaserScan` | `sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan` |
| — its point cloud | | `gz.msgs.PointCloudPacked` | `sensor_msgs/msg/PointCloud2` |
| Depth camera | `depth_camera` | `gz.msgs.Image` | `sensor_msgs/msg/Image` |
| — its point cloud | | `gz.msgs.PointCloudPacked` | `sensor_msgs/msg/PointCloud2` |
| — its intrinsics | | `gz.msgs.CameraInfo` | `sensor_msgs/msg/CameraInfo` |
| IMU | `imu` | `gz.msgs.IMU` | `sensor_msgs/msg/Imu` |

Copy the capitalisation exactly. The IMU pairing is `gz.msgs.IMU` against `sensor_msgs/msg/Imu`, and
a bridge argument that disagrees with the table on one letter starts cleanly and delivers nothing.

One more thing is required that was not before. Sensors are produced by Gazebo *systems*, and
Gazebo's shipped `empty.sdf` loads neither of the two that matter. Rendering-based sensors — LiDAR
and cameras — need `gz-sim-sensors-system`, and IMUs need `gz-sim-imu-system`. A world without them
loads your sensor definitions, reports no error, and publishes nothing, which is the most confusing
possible failure. So this chapter uses its own world.

## Running it

### Environment

ROS 2 **Jazzy Jalisco** and Gazebo **Harmonic** on **Ubuntu 24.04 (Noble)**, installed exactly as in
[Chapter 2.1](./gazebo-setup-and-basics.mdx):

```bash
sudo apt update
sudo apt install ros-jazzy-ros-gz
```

Source every terminal with `source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash`. You need `simple_humanoid.urdf` from
[Chapter 1.4](../ros2/urdf-describing-a-humanoid.mdx) at `~/humanoid/simple_humanoid.urdf`, and the
spawn-and-bridge sequence from Chapter 2.1, used here unchanged.

The SDFormat version Harmonic speaks is **1.11**, which is what the world file below declares and
what every SDF element name in this chapter was checked against.

**This chapter needs working GPU rendering**, as the opening said. `gz-sim-sensors-system` renders
the depth camera and casts the LiDAR's rays through OGRE 2, both on the graphics hardware. On a
machine with software rendering only, the physics and the IMU still work and the two rendering
sensors will be slow or silent — the module landing page's headless route covers the IMU but not
these two.

### Example 1: mounting three sensors

Copy the humanoid and edit the copy — the original stays the reference model:

```bash
cp ~/humanoid/simple_humanoid.urdf ~/humanoid/sensor_humanoid.urdf
```

Paste the block below into `sensor_humanoid.urdf` immediately before the closing `</robot>` tag.

`<gazebo>` is not a URDF element. It is an extension the URDF-to-SDF converter understands: content
inside `<gazebo reference="some_link">` is inserted into the SDF `<link>` of that name. `urdfdom` —
and therefore `check_urdf` and `urdf_tree.py` from Chapter 1.4 — ignores it entirely, so this file is
still a valid URDF describing the same thirteen links and 34.7 kg.

```xml
  <!-- ============ sensors ============ -->

  <!-- IMU in the pelvis, at base_link's origin: near the centre of mass, so
       it measures the body rather than a limb. Noise figures are typical of
       a small solid-state part. Note the form: <noise type="gaussian"> is an ATTRIBUTE
       here, because the IMU reuses SDF's shared noise element. -->
  <gazebo reference="base_link">
    <sensor name="pelvis_imu" type="imu">
      <pose>0 0 0 0 0 0</pose>
      <always_on>1</always_on>
      <update_rate>100</update_rate>
      <topic>humanoid/imu</topic>
      <gz_frame_id>base_link</gz_frame_id>
      <imu>
        <angular_velocity>
          <x><noise type="gaussian">
            <mean>0.0</mean><stddev>0.0002</stddev>
            <bias_mean>0.0000075</bias_mean><bias_stddev>0.0000008</bias_stddev>
          </noise></x>
          <y><noise type="gaussian">
            <mean>0.0</mean><stddev>0.0002</stddev>
            <bias_mean>0.0000075</bias_mean><bias_stddev>0.0000008</bias_stddev>
          </noise></y>
          <z><noise type="gaussian">
            <mean>0.0</mean><stddev>0.0002</stddev>
            <bias_mean>0.0000075</bias_mean><bias_stddev>0.0000008</bias_stddev>
          </noise></z>
        </angular_velocity>
        <linear_acceleration>
          <x><noise type="gaussian">
            <mean>0.0</mean><stddev>0.017</stddev>
            <bias_mean>0.1</bias_mean><bias_stddev>0.001</bias_stddev>
          </noise></x>
          <y><noise type="gaussian">
            <mean>0.0</mean><stddev>0.017</stddev>
            <bias_mean>0.1</bias_mean><bias_stddev>0.001</bias_stddev>
          </noise></y>
          <z><noise type="gaussian">
            <mean>0.0</mean><stddev>0.017</stddev>
            <bias_mean>0.1</bias_mean><bias_stddev>0.001</bias_stddev>
          </noise></z>
        </linear_acceleration>
      </imu>
    </sensor>
  </gazebo>

  <!-- LiDAR on the chest. The torso frame is at the waist, so 0.32 m up is
       chest height; 0.10 m forward clears the 0.18 m deep torso box. A flat
       180-degree sweep of 360 beams at 10 Hz. Here <noise> takes <type> as a
       CHILD element, because the lidar declares its own. -->
  <gazebo reference="torso">
    <sensor name="chest_lidar" type="gpu_lidar">
      <pose>0.10 0 0.32 0 0 0</pose>
      <always_on>1</always_on>
      <update_rate>10</update_rate>
      <visualize>true</visualize>
      <topic>humanoid/scan</topic>
      <gz_frame_id>torso</gz_frame_id>
      <lidar>
        <scan>
          <horizontal>
            <samples>360</samples>
            <resolution>1</resolution>
            <min_angle>-1.570796</min_angle>
            <max_angle>1.570796</max_angle>
          </horizontal>
          <vertical>
            <samples>1</samples>
            <resolution>1</resolution>
            <min_angle>0</min_angle>
            <max_angle>0</max_angle>
          </vertical>
        </scan>
        <range>
          <min>0.20</min>
          <max>12.0</max>
          <resolution>0.01</resolution>
        </range>
        <noise>
          <type>gaussian</type>
          <mean>0.0</mean>
          <stddev>0.01</stddev>
        </noise>
      </lidar>
    </sensor>
  </gazebo>

  <!-- Depth camera in the head, just in front of the visor, whose front face
       is at x = 0.115. A camera looks along +x of its own frame, and the
       head's +x is forward, so no rotation is needed. Deliberately
       noiseless: see the exercises. -->
  <gazebo reference="head">
    <sensor name="head_depth" type="depth_camera">
      <pose>0.13 0 0.13 0 0 0</pose>
      <always_on>1</always_on>
      <update_rate>15</update_rate>
      <topic>humanoid/depth</topic>
      <gz_frame_id>head</gz_frame_id>
      <camera>
        <horizontal_fov>1.047</horizontal_fov>
        <image>
          <width>320</width>
          <height>240</height>
        </image>
        <clip>
          <near>0.10</near>
          <far>10.0</far>
        </clip>
      </camera>
    </sensor>
  </gazebo>
```

Two details in there are worth stopping on, because they cost people an hour each.

**The two noise forms are different.** The LiDAR writes `<noise><type>gaussian</type>...</noise>`,
with `type` as a child element, because SDFormat's `lidar` element declares its own noise block. The
IMU writes `<noise type="gaussian">...</noise>`, with `type` as an **attribute**, because it reuses
SDFormat's shared `noise` element. Neither form works in the other's place. (Cameras use the LiDAR's
form.)

**`<gz_frame_id>` is a Gazebo extension, not part of the SDFormat specification.** It sets the
`header.frame_id` of the published message, and libsdformat will warn that the element is undefined
while Gazebo honours it anyway. It is included here because "which frame is this in?" should be
answerable from the file rather than by experiment — but check what actually arrives, which is what
the node in Example 2 prints.

Now the world. Chapter 2.2's `physics_lab.sdf` plus the two sensor systems, plus something to
measure: a wall whose near face is at exactly *x* = 3.0 m. Save as `sensor_world.sdf`:

Start it, spawn into it, and confirm the sensors exist on the Gazebo side before involving ROS 2 at
all — that is step one of Chapter 2.1's three-step diagnosis, and doing it first saves you from
debugging a bridge that is not the problem:

```bash
gz sim -v 4 sensor_world.sdf
```

```bash
ros2 run ros_gz_sim create \
  -world sensor_lab \
  -file ~/humanoid/sensor_humanoid.urdf \
  -name simple_humanoid \
  -z 0.9
```

```bash
gz topic -l | grep humanoid
```

You should see six topics from three `<topic>` settings:

```
/humanoid/camera_info
/humanoid/depth
/humanoid/depth/points
/humanoid/imu
/humanoid/scan
/humanoid/scan/points
```

Read that list carefully, because two entries in it are the ones people misremember. The LiDAR
advertises `/humanoid/scan/points` as well as `/humanoid/scan`, and the depth camera's intrinsics
land on `/humanoid/camera_info` — a *sibling* of `/humanoid/depth`, for the reason given earlier,
and not the `/humanoid/depth/camera_info` you would guess.

If `grep` finds nothing at all, the sensors did not load: check that you edited the *copy* you
spawned, and that the world is `sensor_world.sdf` and not `empty.sdf`.

### Example 2: one node, three sensors

[Chapter 1.3](../ros2/python-agents-rclpy.mdx)'s node skeleton, unchanged: three subscriptions in
one node, three summary numbers, no fusion — the readings are reported side
by side and never combined, because combining them is Module 3's subject.

- **LiDAR** → the nearest valid return and its bearing. "Nearest" needs the `inf` and
  out-of-range filtering described above, which is most of the LiDAR code.
- **Depth camera** → the centre pixel, in metres, unpacked from the raw `32FC1` bytes with the
  standard library's `struct`. No image library is needed to read one pixel.
- **IMU** → the magnitude of the acceleration vector, plus the smallest and largest seen so far. The
  running extremes matter: the most interesting values occur during a 0.1 s free fall and a
  millisecond-scale impact, and a report every half second would miss both.

Save as `sensor_probe.py`:

```python
"""Reads one summary value from each of the humanoid's three simulated sensors."""

import math
import struct

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node

from sensor_msgs.msg import Image, Imu, LaserScan

SCAN_TOPIC = '/humanoid/scan'
DEPTH_TOPIC = '/humanoid/depth'
IMU_TOPIC = '/humanoid/imu'

REPORT_PERIOD = 0.5      # s of sim time between report lines
DEPTH_ENCODING = '32FC1'  # 32-bit float, one channel, metres


class SensorProbe(Node):

    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('sensor_probe')

        self.create_subscription(LaserScan, SCAN_TOPIC, self.on_scan, 10)
        self.create_subscription(Image, DEPTH_TOPIC, self.on_depth, 10)
        self.create_subscription(Imu, IMU_TOPIC, self.on_imu, 10)
        self.create_timer(REPORT_PERIOD, self.on_report)

        self.counts = {'scan': 0, 'depth': 0, 'imu': 0}
        self.frames = {}

        self.nearest = None        # (range m, bearing deg) or None
        self.centre_depth = None   # m, or None for no return
        self.accel = None          # |a| in m/s^2
        self.accel_low = None
        self.accel_high = None

        self.get_logger().info(
            'waiting for %s, %s and %s'
            % (SCAN_TOPIC, DEPTH_TOPIC, IMU_TOPIC))

    def note_frame(self, key, header):
        """Report each sensor's frame_id once, read off the wire."""
        if key not in self.frames:
            self.frames[key] = header.frame_id
            self.get_logger().info(
                '%s data arrives in frame %r' % (key, header.frame_id))

    def on_scan(self, msg):
        self.counts['scan'] += 1
        self.note_frame('scan', msg.header)

        best = None
        for index, distance in enumerate(msg.ranges):
            # A beam that hit nothing reports inf.
            if not math.isfinite(distance):
                continue
            # Gazebo already clamps finite ranges into [range_min,
            # range_max], so this never fires here. A real driver makes
            # no such promise, so keep it.
            if distance < msg.range_min or distance > msg.range_max:
                continue
            if best is None or distance < best[0]:
                best = (distance, msg.angle_min + index * msg.angle_increment)

        self.nearest = None if best is None else (best[0],
                                                  math.degrees(best[1]))

    def on_depth(self, msg):
        self.counts['depth'] += 1
        self.note_frame('depth', msg.header)

        if msg.encoding != DEPTH_ENCODING:
            self.get_logger().warning(
                'expected %s depth, got %r' % (DEPTH_ENCODING, msg.encoding))
            return

        row = msg.height // 2
        column = msg.width // 2
        offset = row * msg.step + column * 4
        layout = '>f' if msg.is_bigendian else '<f'
        value, = struct.unpack_from(layout, msg.data, offset)
        self.centre_depth = value if math.isfinite(value) else None

    def on_imu(self, msg):
        self.counts['imu'] += 1
        self.note_frame('imu', msg.header)

        a = msg.linear_acceleration
        magnitude = math.sqrt(a.x * a.x + a.y * a.y + a.z * a.z)
        self.accel = magnitude
        if self.accel_low is None or magnitude < self.accel_low:
            self.accel_low = magnitude
        if self.accel_high is None or magnitude > self.accel_high:
            self.accel_high = magnitude

    def on_report(self):
        if not any(self.counts.values()):
            self.get_logger().warning(
                'no sensor messages yet -- is the world running, and is the '
                'bridge started?')
            return

        if self.nearest is None:
            scan_text = 'lidar nothing in range'
        else:
            scan_text = 'lidar %.3f m at %+.1f deg' % self.nearest

        if self.centre_depth is None:
            depth_text = 'depth centre no return'
        else:
            depth_text = 'depth centre %.3f m' % self.centre_depth

        if self.accel is None:
            imu_text = 'imu no data'
        else:
            imu_text = ('imu |a| %5.2f (seen %.2f..%.2f) m/s2'
                        % (self.accel, self.accel_low, self.accel_high))

        self.get_logger().info(
            '%s | %s | %s | msgs %d/%d/%d'
            % (scan_text, depth_text, imu_text,
               self.counts['scan'], self.counts['depth'],
               self.counts['imu']))


def main(args=None):
    rclpy.init(args=args)
    node = SensorProbe()
    rclpy.spin(node)
    node.destroy_node()
    rclpy.shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

Nothing arrives yet: the world is paused, no steps are being taken, and a sensor with no steps has
nothing to sample. Press **Play** and read the sequence.

**The first report, while the robot is still upright.** This is the one to check against the world
file, and it is the whole reason a wall was put at a known distance:

- The LiDAR sits at *x* ≈ 0.10 m and the wall's face at *x* = 3.0 m, so the nearest return should be
  about **2.90 m at about 0 degrees** — jittering by a centimetre or so, because you gave it 1 cm of
  Gaussian noise.
- The depth camera sits 3 cm further forward, so its centre pixel should read about **2.87 m**, and
  should not jitter at all, because you gave it no noise.
- `|a|` should be near 9.8 but not equal to it: about **9.90**, or about **9.70**. The robot is
  standing still and its accelerometer is reading gravity upward, so 9.8 is the true answer — and
  the offset is the `bias_mean` of 0.1 you gave each acceleration axis. Gazebo draws that bias once
  when the sensor loads and, by convention, flips its sign with equal probability, so the vertical
  axis settles on 9.9 or 9.7 and holds it for the whole run:
  sqrt(0.1² + 0.1² + 9.9²) ≈ 9.90. Restart the world a few times and watch it flip. A constant
  offset that averaging cannot remove and that differs on each power-up is exactly what `bias_mean`
  models, and this is the one number in the chapter you should *not* expect to match the world file.

If the two distances differ by much more than the 3 cm their `<pose>` values predict, something is
wrong with a `<pose>`, not with the sensors.

**Then the fall from Chapter 2.2, in three signals.** The robot was spawned 5 cm above the ground:

- During the drop, `|a|` goes to nearly **zero** — about 0.17, not 0.00, because the three biases
  are still there and nothing else is. This is the free-fall result from earlier in the
  chapter, and it is the single most useful thing this example shows you: an accelerometer in free
  fall reads nothing. The `seen` low-water mark captures it even if no report line lands inside the
  0.1 s window.
- At impact `|a|` spikes far above 9.8 — the ground stopping 34.7 kg in a few milliseconds. The
  `seen` high-water mark captures that too.
- As the humanoid collapses, the chest LiDAR and head camera pitch toward the floor. The nearest
  return collapses from 2.90 m to something short, and the depth centre pixel follows or drops to
  *no return* as the camera ends up looking at the sky.

Once it has settled, `|a|` returns to whichever of those two rest values it started at — the robot
is at rest again, with the same biases it had — but the *direction*
of that vector has changed, which is exactly the tilt signal Chapter 2.2 said a balance controller
would need and did not have. This chapter does not build that controller. It has given it something
to read.

If a report line shows `msgs 0/0/N` or similar, one bridge argument is wrong while the others are
fine: check that pairing against the table above, letter by letter, before checking anything else.

## Exercises

Each needs the environment above, `sensor_humanoid.urdf`, `sensor_world.sdf`, and `sensor_probe.py`.

**1. Find the frames without being told.** Before reading `sensor_probe.py`'s frame lines, predict
what `header.frame_id` each sensor will carry. Then run it, and also check `ros2 topic echo
/humanoid/imu --field header.frame_id` and `ros2 topic info /humanoid/scan`.
*Correct result*: the three `<gz_frame_id>` values you wrote — `base_link`, `torso`, `head` — and
`ros2 topic info` reporting type `sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan` with one publisher, the bridge. Then
delete the `<gz_frame_id>` line from one sensor, respawn, and record what arrives instead. Say why
publishing readings with a wrong `frame_id` is worse than publishing none.

**2. Make the noise visible, then take it away.** Watch four or five report lines with the robot
upright and write down the spread in the LiDAR's nearest range. Then set the LiDAR's `<stddev>` to
`0.0`, respawn, and compare. Then set it to `0.10` and compare again.
*Correct result*: roughly ±1–3 cm of scatter at `0.01`, a value that stops moving at `0.0`, and
scatter of order ±10–30 cm at `0.10`, at which point the nearest return is occasionally a hard
**0.20 m** from some beam that is not the one pointing at the wall. That exact figure is the
LiDAR's own `<min>`, and it is not a coincidence: Gazebo applies the noise first and *then* clamps
every finite range into `[range_min, range_max]`, so a strongly negative draw does not arrive as an
arbitrary short reading — it arrives as precisely the minimum range. Say what that does to code
that steers away from `nearest`, and what you would add to `on_scan` to defend against it.

**3. Move the camera and predict the number.** The depth camera is at `0.13 0 0.13` in the head
frame. Change its `<pose>` to `0.13 0 0.13 0 0.35 0` — pitched down by 0.35 rad — respawn, and
predict the centre-pixel reading before you look. You will need the camera's height above the
ground, which you can add up from Chapter 1.4's joint origins.
*Correct result*: standing, `base_link` is 0.85 m up, the torso frame 0.07 m above that, the head
frame 0.42 m above that, and the camera 0.13 m above that — **1.47 m**. The wall face is 2.87 m
ahead, and a downward pitch of 0.35 rad would meet the floor only at 1.47 / tan(0.35) ≈ 4.0 m of
horizontal distance, so the centre ray still lands on the wall, at about 2.87 / cos(0.35) ≈
**3.06 m**. Check the sign of your rotation: if the reading gets *shorter* rather than longer, you
pitched the camera up. Say why the LiDAR's reading is unaffected by this edit.

**4. Watch the sensor rates disagree with the clock.** Let the simulation run for a known stretch of
simulated time and compare each sensor's message count against its `<update_rate>`. Then set the
world's `max_step_size` to `0.0001` and repeat.
*Correct result*: counts in the ratio 100 : 15 : 10 for IMU : depth : LiDAR, matching the update
rates, and after the change the same counts per *simulated* second while the real-time factor falls
by roughly ten — Chapter 2.2's step-size trade-off, now visible in sensor data. Say what this means
for a perception node that assumes a fixed wall-clock interval between frames, and which of the two
clocks such a node should be reading.

---

The humanoid now produces data of the same shape a real robot's would: distances in a frame, a depth
image in metres, and a gravity vector that says which way is down. Three separate answers to three
separate questions, none of which agrees with the others about anything yet — the LiDAR has no
opinion about tilt, and the IMU has never heard of the wall.

Turning three partial views into one estimate of where the robot is and what is around it is sensor
fusion, and it is what the next module is for. Before that, one more chapter in this module: a second
renderer, for the times when the audience for the simulation is a human rather than a physics
engine.
