# Physics, Gravity & Collisions

At the end of [Chapter 2.1](./gazebo-setup-and-basics.mdx) you pressed Play and the robot fell down.

That is the correct outcome, and worth more than a working demo, because it is the first time
anything in this book has *disagreed* with you. Until Chapter 2.1, nothing had tested a single claim
in `simple_humanoid.urdf`. A physics engine tests all of them several thousand times a second, and
takes every number far more literally than you meant it.

This chapter is about what happens in each of those one-millisecond steps, and about the two
separate reasons — not one — that the humanoid ends up on the floor. Neither is a bug.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Explain why the book's humanoid falls over, in terms of what the physics engine computes rather
  than which parameter is wrong.
- Identify which physical property — mass, the inertia tensor, the centre of mass, friction,
  collision geometry — to change in order to affect a given behaviour, and predict the direction of
  the change.
- Describe what shrinking the physics step buys you and what it costs.
- Measure a fall programmatically from a Python ROS 2 node instead of judging it by eye.

## What the engine actually computes

A physics engine solves **forward dynamics**: given the state of every body and the forces on it,
find the state a moment later. It cannot do that in closed form for a system with contacts, so it
does it in slices. One slice is one **step**, and `empty.sdf` sets that slice to 1 ms.

Each step runs the same five stages.

1. **Collect state.** Position, orientation, linear and angular velocity for all thirteen links.
2. **Apply external forces.** Gravity, mostly — one force per link, proportional to its mass.
3. **Collision detection.** Find every pair of collision shapes that overlap or are about to, and
   where.
4. **Constraint solving.** The expensive stage, and the interesting one. The engine holds a list of
   things that must remain true — the knee's two links stay connected and rotate only about the knee
   axis; the foot does not sink into the ground; the foot does not slide unless the tangential force
   exceeds what friction can resist — and solves for the forces making all of them true
   *simultaneously*. Joints and contacts go into the same solve, which is why a bad joint and a bad
   contact produce similarly strange results.
5. **Integrate.** Advance velocities by the accelerations just computed, positions by the velocities,
   and stop.

Then do it again. Gazebo Harmonic's default engine for this is **DART**, loaded as
`gz-physics-dartsim-plugin`; `gz sim --physics-engine gz-physics-bullet-featherstone-plugin` swaps
it. The choice changes numbers at the margins and is not this chapter's subject — every engine runs
those five stages, and everything below is about what *you* hand them.

Notice what is absent. Nothing in that list decides what the robot should *do*. There is no stage
where a knee is held at an angle, because holding a knee at an angle is a force, and no force in
this simulation has an author.

## Mass, inertia, and the centre of mass

Three quantities, easy to blur together, that do different jobs in stage 4.

**Mass** resists linear acceleration: *F* = *ma*. The humanoid's thirteen links sum to 34.7 kg, so
under gravity it is pulled down by 34.7 × 9.8 = **340 N**, and 340 N is what its two feet have to
push back with to hold it up.

**The inertia tensor** is the rotational counterpart: how hard the link is to *spin up* about each
axis. [Chapter 1.4](../ros2/urdf-describing-a-humanoid.mdx) introduced the six numbers and the box
and sphere formulas they came from. Until now they have been XML. In stage 4 they become the matrix
the solver inverts to turn a torque into an angular acceleration, and that is the whole reason a
wrong one is catastrophic rather than merely inaccurate — the tensor is not a value the engine
reports back to you, it is a value the engine *divides by*.

**The centre of mass** is where the link's `<inertial><origin>` puts it, which is a separate choice
from where the visual shape is. Chapter 1.4's torso illustrates it: the link frame sits at the
waist, and the inertial origin is offset to `0 0 0.21` to put the mass where the chest actually is.
Get that offset wrong and the mass is right, the shape is right, and the robot still leans.

For the whole robot it is the mass-weighted average of the links'. Summing Chapter 1.4's thirteen
`<inertial>` blocks with every joint at zero puts the humanoid's centre of mass at **x = 0.002 m,
y = 0.000 m, z = 0.007 m** in the `base_link` frame — almost exactly at the pelvis, where a roughly
human mass distribution should put it. The soles sit 0.85 m below `base_link`, so it stands
**0.857 m above the ground**. Hold on to that number; the second half of the chapter is about it.

### Why a wrong inertia tensor makes a robot absurd

The single most common way to break a simulated robot is a unit slip. CAD packages report inertia
in kg·mm²; URDF wants kg·m². The conversion factor is 10⁶, and pasting the CAD figure without it
makes every tensor a million times too large.

The torso's real `izz` is 0.1108 kg·m², so 1 N·m about its vertical axis gives 1 / 0.1108 ≈ 9 rad/s².
With the CAD figure pasted in, `izz` is 110800 kg·m² and the same torque produces 9 × 10⁻⁶ rad/s².
The torso does not become heavy; it becomes *unturnable* — and the failure is peculiar because
everything else still looks right. It is 12 kg, gravity pulls on it correctly, it falls at 9.8 m/s².
It simply refuses to rotate while doing so, and a robot whose torso will not turn but whose arms will
behaves in a way no real object does.

The error runs the other way too, and worse. Too *small* a tensor makes the link a hair trigger,
spun violently by a small contact impulse — and since contact impulses arrive every step, that
usually means the robot explodes on the first frame. The pathological case is zero inertia, or an
`<inertial>` block omitted. Chapter 1.4 noted the parser treats `<inertial>` as optional; it is
optional to the *parser*. To the solver a zero-inertia link is a division by zero dressed up as a
robot part.

The rule this gives you is the diagnostic habit the rest of the module depends on:

> If a body **translates** wrongly, suspect mass, gravity, or contact. If a body **rotates**
> wrongly — too fast, too slow, or about an axis that makes no sense — suspect the inertia tensor.
> If a body is wrongly *placed* while moving plausibly, suspect the inertial origin.

## Gravity

Gravity is a property of the world, not of the robot. In `empty.sdf` it is one line:

```xml
<gravity>0 0 -9.8</gravity>
```

Three components in m/s² along the world axes. Set it to `0 0 0` and the humanoid hangs where you
spawned it; `0 0 -1.62` gives you the Moon.

Be precise about gravity's role in the fall, because it is easy to blame it and stop thinking.
Gravity does not make the robot unstable. It makes the instability *visible* — a constant, uniform
340 N of load that finds every direction the structure cannot resist in. Turn gravity off and the
humanoid does not become balanced; it becomes untested.

## Collision and contact

Stage 3 answers "what is touching what". Naively that is *n*²/2 shape-pair checks, so engines split
it: a **broad phase** discarding pairs that cheap bounding volumes prove cannot touch, and a
**narrow phase** computing exact contact points for the survivors. That split is why a robot of
simple primitives simulates fast and one wearing a 200,000-triangle mesh does not.

Stage 4 answers "what does touching *do*". A contact becomes a constraint: the shapes must not
interpenetrate, so the solver finds the normal impulse cancelling the approaching velocity. It is a
constraint, not a spring — the engine is not pushing the foot out of the ground, it is refusing to
let it in. A little interpenetration is tolerated and corrected over later steps, because forbidding
it strictly produces a stiff problem needing tiny steps; that is why a resting foot in a simulator
sits a fraction of a millimetre inside the floor rather than exactly on it.

Two consequences you meet immediately:

**The engine sees `<collision>`, never `<visual>`.** Chapter 1.4 made the head's collision sphere
`0.12` against the skull's visual `0.11`, and gave it no visor at all. This robot's head contacts a
doorframe a centimetre before the picture touches, and its visor clips straight through with no
contact at all. Picture and physics are independent statements; only one is enforced.

**Adjacent links do not collide.** Links joined by a joint have their collision checks disabled by
default — otherwise thigh and shin, sharing a surface at the knee, would be in permanent violent
contact. The cost is that the humanoid's arm passes through its own torso, since self-collision is
off by default for the whole model and enabling it is expensive.

## Friction

The normal impulse stops the foot sinking. Nothing yet stops it sliding.

Friction is the tangential half of the contact constraint, and engines implement Coulomb's model:
the tangential force available is at most **μ** times the normal force — below that the contact
sticks, at it the contact slides. Since μ describes a *pair* of surfaces and the engine knows only
each shape separately, it combines the two values, so the friction under the humanoid's foot depends
on the foot *and* the ground plane. In SDF it lives inside the collision element:

```xml
<collision name="collision">
  <geometry>
    <box><size>0.22 0.10 0.06</size></box>
  </geometry>
  <surface>
    <friction>
      <ode>
        <mu>1.0</mu>
        <mu2>1.0</mu2>
      </ode>
    </friction>
  </surface>
</collision>
```

`mu` and `mu2` are the coefficients along two perpendicular tangential directions — the friction
*pyramid*, a linear approximation of the true friction cone, used because a pyramid fits the
solver's linear machinery and a cone does not. Both default to **1.0**, a fairly grippy surface,
roughly rubber on concrete; setting `mu` ≠ `mu2` is how you model a wheel or a ski.

Chapter 1.4's URDF specifies no friction anywhere, so every contact in Chapter 2.1 used defaults on
both sides. Know that before you conclude anything about the fall: μ = 1.0 under both feet is
generous, and the robot still went down. Friction is not what is missing.

## Why the biped falls, and what that reveals

Now the chapter's real question. The humanoid is dropped 5 cm onto a high-friction floor and
collapses. There are **two independent failures** here, and readers usually see only one of them,
which is why "add a controller" tends to be the wrong first move.

### Failure one: nothing holds the joints

Chapter 1.4 gave `l_knee_joint` an `effort="100.0"`. It is easy to read that as a motor. It is not.
In URDF, `effort` is a *limit* — a ceiling on what an actuator may command, a statement about a
motor that might exist. It commands nothing.

Chapter 2.1 already told you the mechanical reason this stays true in simulation: the URDF carries
no `<gazebo>` tags, so no Gazebo system attaches to the model, so nothing is driving any joint. In
stage 4 the knee constraint says only *these two links stay connected and rotate about this axis*.
It says nothing about which angle. A joint with no actuator, no damping and no friction is a
frictionless hinge, and thirteen links on twelve frictionless hinges is not a robot standing up. It
is a chain, and a chain under gravity finds the floor.

This is the failure that dominates the first half-second, and it is a *postural* failure, not a
balance failure. Even a robot with perfect balance would still be a puddle if its joints were free.

### Failure two: it would tip over anyway

Suppose you fixed failure one — welded every joint, so the humanoid became one rigid 34.7 kg body
in exactly the pose Chapter 1.4 describes. It would still fall, and this second failure is the one
that generalises.

A body standing on the ground is stable only while the vertical line through its centre of mass
lands inside its **support polygon** — the convex hull of everything touching the ground. Compute
both for this robot from Chapter 1.4's numbers.

The foot collision boxes are 0.22 × 0.10 × 0.06 m, offset `0.04` forward in each ankle frame, with
the ankles at y = ±0.09. So the support polygon spans **x from −0.070 to +0.150 m** and **y from
−0.140 to +0.140 m**. The centre of mass sits at x = 0.002, y = 0.000, and 0.857 m up. That leaves:

| Direction | Margin | Lean before tipping |
|---|---|---|
| Backwards | 0.072 m | **4.8°** |
| Forwards | 0.148 m | 9.8° |
| Sideways | 0.140 m | 9.3° |

Two things fall out of that table. The margins are tiny — under five degrees backwards. And they are
**asymmetric**, not arbitrarily: the feet project forward of the ankle, exactly as human feet do,
which is what makes the forward margin twice the backward one. You did not design that in Chapter
1.4; it fell out of a `0.04` offset on a collision box. Physical properties chosen for one reason
determine behaviour you were not thinking about.

Now the part that makes balance hard rather than merely fiddly. A body balanced on a small base with
its mass high above it is an **inverted pendulum**, whose upright position is an *unstable*
equilibrium: any lean produces a gravitational torque that increases the lean. The deviation grows
exponentially, with a time constant of

  τ = √(L / g) = √(0.857 / 9.8) = **0.30 s**

Start it one degree off vertical and it reaches the 4.8° backward tipping limit in about **0.67 s**.
That number is the whole problem. It is not that the robot is badly built; it is that a humanoid has
roughly two thirds of a second to notice an error and correct it, and the window shrinks as the
error grows. Every "balance" technique you will meet later — measuring the tilt, moving the centre
of pressure inside the foot, taking a step to put the support polygon back under the falling mass —
is an answer to that arithmetic.

Note that both terms in τ are things you can read off the model. Lower the centre of mass and τ
grows and balancing gets easier, which is why so many real robots carry their batteries in the
shins. Bigger feet enlarge the support polygon and buy margin, which is why humanoid feet look the
way they do.

### What this chapter does not do

It does not balance the robot. Doing so needs two things the book has not built: a way to *measure*
the tilt, which is a sensor and therefore the next chapter's subject, and a way to *apply* torque at
the joints, which is a controller. **Balance is the open problem here, named and left open.** What
you should take away is why it is hard — 0.30 seconds and 4.8 degrees — rather than a parameter to
change.

## The step size trade-off

One number in the world file governs everything above:

```xml
<physics name="default" type="dart">
  <max_step_size>0.001</max_step_size>
  <real_time_factor>1.0</real_time_factor>
</physics>
```

`max_step_size` is the slice of simulated time each step advances, in seconds; Gazebo's shipped
worlds default to 1 ms. Three pressures act on it in different directions.

**Accuracy.** Integration assumes forces are constant across the step. They are not, so each step
accumulates error, and error per simulated second falls as the step shrinks.

**Stability.** The pressure people underestimate. Contact and joint constraints are *stiff* — large
forces over very short distances — and integrating a stiff system with too large a step makes the
error not merely grow but **diverge**: the correction overshoots, the next overshoots further, and
the model detonates. That is why the classic symptom of an overlarge step is not "slightly wrong"
but "the robot vibrates, then leaves the scene".

**Speed.** Cost is per step, so halving `max_step_size` doubles the computation per simulated
second. At 1 ms the engine runs 1000 steps per simulated second; at 0.1 ms, 10,000. A machine
holding real-time factor 1.0 now manages about 0.1 — and
[Chapter 2.1](./gazebo-setup-and-basics.mdx)'s point returns with force, because your nodes with
`use_sim_time: true` do not degrade gracefully, they simply live in a world where a wall-clock
minute buys six simulated seconds. A day-long training run becomes ten days.

So: **shrink the step until the simulation is stable and accurate enough for the question you are
asking, and not one step further.** For a walking humanoid with stiff foot contacts, 1 ms is a
reasonable default and 5 ms is often already too coarse; for a wheeled robot rolling across a flat
floor, 10 ms may be fine. There is no globally correct value, only one that is correct for your
contacts.

`real_time_factor` is a *cap*, not a target: 1.0 asks Gazebo not to outrun reality even if it could,
and `0` removes the cap — what you want for headless data generation, not for watching.

### Example 1: measuring the fall instead of watching it

*Status: pending execution in a Gazebo-capable environment. Syntax-checked with `python -m
py_compile`; uses [Chapter 1.3](../ros2/python-agents-rclpy.mdx)'s node skeleton unchanged.*

"It fell over" is not a measurement, and eyes are poor at telling a robot that crumpled from one
that toppled. This node reads the fall as numbers.

It listens to `/world/empty/pose/info`, the scene broadcaster topic from Chapter 2.1, bridged as
`tf2_msgs/msg/TFMessage` — the pairing you worked out in that chapter's fourth exercise. One detail
governs how to read it: the scene broadcaster publishes each entity's pose **relative to its
parent**, so the *model* entry is in world coordinates while *link* entries are relative to the
model. The node prints each tracked transform's parent frame on arrival so you can confirm that
rather than take it on faith.

That gives two independent numbers, and the pair is the point. The model's **world height** says how
far the robot dropped. The head's height **relative to the model** says whether it kept its shape:
the head frame sits 0.490 m above `base_link` with every joint at zero, and stays there through any
rigid topple, so a shrinking value is the joints buckling. One number separates tipping from
collapsing. Save as `fall_probe.py`:

```python
"""Measures the humanoid's fall numerically from bridged Gazebo pose data."""

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node

from tf2_msgs.msg import TFMessage

POSE_TOPIC = '/world/empty/pose/info'
MODEL = 'simple_humanoid'
HEAD = 'head'

HEAD_REST_Z = 0.490      # m above base_link with every joint at zero
SPAWN_Z = 0.9            # m, the -z given to ros_gz_sim create
REPORT_PERIOD = 0.5      # s of sim time between report lines
SETTLE_WINDOW = 1.0      # s of sim time the height must stay still
SETTLE_TOLERANCE = 0.002  # m


def stamp_seconds(stamp):
    """Convert a builtin_interfaces/msg/Time into float seconds."""
    return stamp.sec + stamp.nanosec * 1e-9


def short_name(frame):
    """'simple_humanoid/base_link' -> 'base_link'; plain names pass through."""
    return frame.rsplit('/', 1)[-1]


class FallProbe(Node):

    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('fall_probe')

        self.create_subscription(TFMessage, POSE_TOPIC, self.on_poses, 10)
        self.create_timer(REPORT_PERIOD, self.on_report)

        self.t_zero = None
        self.t_now = 0.0
        self.world_z = None
        self.head_rel_z = None
        self.lowest_world_z = None
        self.fastest_drop = 0.0
        self.previous = None       # (t, world z) from the last message
        self.history = []          # recent (t, world z) inside the settle window
        self.settled_at = None
        self.announced = set()

        self.get_logger().info(
            'listening on %s -- start the bridge if nothing arrives'
            % POSE_TOPIC)

    def on_poses(self, msg):
        for transform in msg.transforms:
            name = short_name(transform.child_frame_id)
            if name not in (MODEL, HEAD):
                continue

            if name not in self.announced:
                self.announced.add(name)
                self.get_logger().info(
                    'tracking %r, whose pose is expressed in %r'
                    % (transform.child_frame_id, transform.header.frame_id))

            t = stamp_seconds(transform.header.stamp)
            if self.t_zero is None:
                self.t_zero = t
            self.t_now = t - self.t_zero

            z = transform.transform.translation.z
            if name == HEAD:
                self.head_rel_z = z
            else:
                self.track_model(self.t_now, z)

    def track_model(self, t, z):
        self.world_z = z
        if self.lowest_world_z is None or z < self.lowest_world_z:
            self.lowest_world_z = z

        if self.previous is not None:
            dt = t - self.previous[0]
            if dt > 0.0:
                rate = (z - self.previous[1]) / dt
                self.fastest_drop = min(self.fastest_drop, rate)
        self.previous = (t, z)

        self.history.append((t, z))
        while self.history and t - self.history[0][0] > SETTLE_WINDOW:
            self.history.pop(0)
        self.check_settled(t)

    def check_settled(self, t):
        if self.settled_at is not None or len(self.history) < 2:
            return
        if t - self.history[0][0] < SETTLE_WINDOW:
            return

        heights = [h for _, h in self.history]
        if max(heights) - min(heights) > SETTLE_TOLERANCE:
            return

        self.settled_at = t
        deviation = abs(self.head_rel_z - HEAD_REST_Z) \
            if self.head_rel_z is not None else float('nan')
        self.get_logger().info(
            'SETTLED at sim t %.2f s | world z %.3f m | dropped %.3f m from '
            'spawn | fastest descent %.2f m/s | head rel z %.3f m '
            '(deviation %.3f m)'
            % (t, self.world_z, SPAWN_Z - self.world_z, self.fastest_drop,
               self.head_rel_z if self.head_rel_z is not None else float('nan'),
               deviation))

    def on_report(self):
        if self.world_z is None:
            self.get_logger().warning('no poses yet on %s' % POSE_TOPIC)
            return
        if self.settled_at is not None:
            return

        head = self.head_rel_z if self.head_rel_z is not None else float('nan')
        self.get_logger().info(
            't %5.2f s | world z %.3f m | lowest %.3f m | head rel z %.3f m '
            '(rest %.3f)'
            % (self.t_now, self.world_z, self.lowest_world_z, head,
               HEAD_REST_Z))


def main(args=None):
    rclpy.init(args=args)
    node = FallProbe()
    rclpy.spin(node)
    node.destroy_node()
    rclpy.shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

  you check that claim instead of believing it. A rigid topple leaves it at 0.490, so any sustained
  departure is the joints giving way — failure one, as a measurement.
- **SETTLED** prints once the world height has held within 2 mm for a simulated second. Write down
  the settled world z and the head deviation; Example 2 changes one number in the model and asks
  what happens to that pair.

### Example 2: breaking the inertia tensor on purpose

*Status: the `scale_inertia.py` script below was executed against Chapter 1.4's
`simple_humanoid.urdf` and its output is reproduced as printed, except that the final line names
whatever output path you pass it. The Gazebo run that consumes the result is pending execution in a
Gazebo-capable environment.*

Now cause the CAD unit slip deliberately and measure it. This script copies a URDF and multiplies
one link's six inertia terms by a factor, leaving everything else — mass, geometry, joints, the
inertial origin — untouched. It uses the same `xml.etree.ElementTree` approach as Chapter 1.4's
`urdf_tree.py`, so nothing here is new. Save as `scale_inertia.py`:

```python
"""Copy a URDF, scaling one link's inertia tensor by a factor."""

import sys
import xml.etree.ElementTree as ET

TENSOR = ('ixx', 'ixy', 'ixz', 'iyy', 'iyz', 'izz')


def scale_inertia(in_path, out_path, link_name, factor):
    tree = ET.parse(in_path)
    for link in tree.getroot().findall('link'):
        if link.get('name') != link_name:
            continue
        inertia = link.find('inertial/inertia')
        if inertia is None:
            raise SystemExit('link %r has no inertia element' % link_name)
        print('%s, inertia x %g:' % (link_name, factor))
        for key in TENSOR:
            scaled = float(inertia.get(key)) * factor
            inertia.set(key, '%.6g' % scaled)
            print('  %s = %s' % (key, inertia.get(key)))
        tree.write(out_path, xml_declaration=True, encoding='utf-8')
        print('wrote %s' % out_path)
        return
    raise SystemExit('no link named %r in %s' % (link_name, in_path))


def main(argv):
    if len(argv) != 5:
        raise SystemExit('usage: scale_inertia.py IN.urdf OUT.urdf LINK FACTOR')
    scale_inertia(argv[1], argv[2], argv[3], float(argv[4]))


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main(sys.argv)
```

Run it on the torso with the factor a millimetre-to-metre slip would give you:

Compare the two settled reports, and reason about what you expect *before* you look — the prediction
is the exercise. The free-fall phase should be identical, since mass and gravity are untouched and
inertia plays no part in translation. Everything after the feet make contact must diverge, because
that is where torques start acting on the torso, and a torso a million times harder to rotate cannot
participate in the crumple. The head-relative-z deviation is the number that carries that; the
settle time is the number that shows the fall taking a different shape.

Then try the error the other way, with `1e-6`, and be ready to stop the simulator quickly. A
near-zero-inertia link driven by ordinary contact impulses is the "model leaves the scene" failure
from earlier, and `fall_probe.py` reports it as a world z of absurd magnitude rather than as
anything you could watch.

## Exercises

Each needs the environment above, both Python programs, and Chapter 1.4's `simple_humanoid.urdf`.

**1. Predict a drop before you run it.** Spawn at `-z 1.5` instead of `0.9` and predict, before

9.8 m/s². Check both against `fall_probe.py`.
*Correct result*: 0.65 m of free fall — about 0.36 s and about 3.6 m/s downward, roughly 3.6 times

**2. Move the mass, and predict the direction.** Adapt `scale_inertia.py` into a script that edits
the *head* link's `<inertial><origin>` from `0 0 0.12` to `0.15 0 0.12`, leaving mass and tensor

spawn it.
*Correct result*: 2.5 kg moving 0.15 m forward shifts the 34.7 kg robot's centre of mass forward by

backward margin grows. The point is that you can predict the *sign and size* from the model alone.
Say why the fall may nevertheless look unchanged, given failure one.

*Correct result*: x from −0.110 to +0.190 and y from −0.160 to +0.160, giving about 7.4° backwards
instead of 4.8° and about 10.6° sideways instead of 9.3°. Then say what τ = √(L/g) does in response,
and why bigger feet therefore buy margin but not time.
