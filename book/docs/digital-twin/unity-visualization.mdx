# Unity for Robot Visualization

[Chapter 2.3](./simulating-sensors.mdx) left the humanoid with three sensors and a node that reads
all three. Every result in that chapter arrived as a line of text: `lidar 2.903 m at +0.3 deg`.
That is the right format for the audience it had — you, checking a number against a wall you placed
at *x* = 3.0 m.

It is the wrong format for a different audience. A safety reviewer asking whether the robot's arm
sweeps into the walkway, a mechanical engineer who wants to see the visor's field of view, a
customer being shown what the machine will do in their building: none of them can read
`lidar 2.903 m at +0.3 deg`, and Gazebo's own viewport — grey boxes on an infinite grey plane — does
not help them much either.

This chapter is about that second audience, and about the honest accounting of what it costs to
serve them. Unity is a second renderer bolted onto a simulation that is otherwise unchanged. It
adds a great deal to what a human can see and **nothing** to what is true.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Decide, for a specific task, whether to reach for Unity or stay with Gazebo — and say what Unity
  does not replace.
- Describe how data crosses from ROS 2 into a Unity scene, and why a TCP endpoint sits in the middle
  rather than Unity speaking ROS 2 directly.
- Write a `rclpy` node that publishes joint angles for this book's humanoid in a form a Unity scene
  can render.
- Name the three conversions that have to happen between a ROS 2 joint angle and a moving Unity
  robot, and which one silently produces a robot that moves 57 times too far.

## Two audiences for one simulation

Every simulator renders, but not for the same reason.

**Gazebo's viewport exists so that you can debug the physics.** It draws what the engine holds:
collision shapes when you ask for them, contact points, the joint frames. Chapter 2.3's LiDAR made
this concrete in an uncomfortable way — physics sees a 0.12 m sphere for the head, the renderer sees
a 0.11 m skull plus an orange visor, and the two disagree by design. That viewport is an instrument.
It is not trying to look like anything.

**Unity's renderer exists so that a person believes what they are looking at.** It is a
general-purpose real-time renderer with the material, lighting, shadow, and post-processing
machinery of a commercial game engine, plus an asset pipeline for importing furnished rooms,
warehouse shelving, and human characters. Pointed at a robot simulation, it produces images that a
non-roboticist can interpret without translation.

Those are different jobs, and the second one is worth naming plainly, because engineers routinely
undervalue it. Robotics projects are funded, staffed, and cancelled by people who will never run
`ros2 topic echo`. A ten-second clip of the humanoid standing in a rendered room does more to
communicate the state of a project than any amount of correct telemetry.

There is a second consumer of good pixels that is not human at all: a perception model trained on
camera images. What that model learns depends on lighting, materials, reflections, and clutter, so
rendering fidelity stops being cosmetic and starts being part of the data. That thread is picked up
in the next module, with a different tool built specifically for it; this chapter stays on the human
audience.

### What Unity does not replace

Unity ships a physics engine. This book does not use it, and the reason is a rule rather than a
preference.

**A simulation gets exactly one authority on what is true.** In this book that is Gazebo, and it has
been since [Chapter 2.1](./gazebo-setup-and-basics.mdx). The masses, inertia tensors, contact
behaviour, and friction that [Chapter 2.2](./physics-gravity-collisions.mdx) spent a chapter
justifying are consumed by Gazebo's solver. Unity's solver is a different implementation, tuned for
different priorities, and it would produce a different fall from the same URDF. Two answers to the
same question is not redundancy — it is a bug you cannot localise. So Unity's rigid-body simulation
stays switched off for the robot, and the Unity scene is downstream: it is *told* where the joints
are, and it never decides.

The same applies to sensors. The `sensor_msgs/msg/LaserScan` your code consumes comes from Gazebo's
ray casts, because those are the numbers that were checked against a wall at a known distance.
Rendering the same scene beautifully in Unity does not make a second LiDAR more correct.

So: **Unity renders, Gazebo rules.** Every design decision in this chapter follows from that.

## When it is worth the toolchain, and when it is not

Unity is a multi-gigabyte install, a second description of your robot to keep in step with the URDF,
a C# codebase, and an extra process on the network. That is a real cost and it is paid continuously,
not once.

Reach for Unity when **the pixels are the product**:

- Someone outside the robotics team has to see the robot work — a demo, a design review, a
  stakeholder update, an operator-facing view.
- The environment itself matters to the question: the robot has to be shown in a furnished room, a
  shop floor, a hospital corridor. Gazebo can hold such a world, but building and lighting it is
  Unity's day job and not Gazebo's.
- You need a camera view that looks like a real camera, for a person to judge.

Stay with Gazebo, and do not install anything, when:

- The question is physical. Does it tip, does the foot slip, is the inertia right. Unity cannot
  answer these, because it is not the authority.
- The question is about frames, transforms, or the model's structure. **RViz already does this**, it
  is already installed, and [Chapter 1.4](../ros2/urdf-describing-a-humanoid.mdx) showed you how to
  drive it. Reaching for Unity to check a TF tree is an expensive way to be slower.
- The question is about data. `ros2 topic echo`, a plot, or Chapter 2.3's probe node answers it in
  seconds and leaves a number you can put in a commit message.

The failure mode worth naming: teams install Unity early because it looks impressive, then maintain
a Unity scene that drifts out of sync with the URDF until nobody trusts it. The scene is not the
robot. Import it from the URDF, re-import when the URDF changes, and never hand-edit the imported
hierarchy — that discipline is most of what keeps a Unity scene worth having.

## How data crosses: why there is a TCP endpoint

[Chapter 1.2](../ros2/ros2-architecture.mdx) established that ROS 2 nodes find each other and
exchange messages over **DDS**, and [Chapter 2.1](./gazebo-setup-and-basics.mdx) established that
Gazebo Transport is a *different* middleware, which is why a bridge process has to translate between
them. Unity is a third case, and a harsher one.

A ROS 2 client library — `rclpy` included — is a wrapper over a C library, `rcl`, which sits on top
of a DDS vendor's implementation, plus generated type-support code for every message type. Making
Unity a DDS participant would mean carrying all of that inside the Editor's managed C# runtime, on
every platform Unity targets, and keeping it in step with each ROS 2 release. Unity Robotics did not
do that.

Instead, the connection is deliberately dumb, and made of two halves:

| Half | Where it runs | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **ROS-TCP-Endpoint** | On the ROS 2 machine | An ordinary ROS 2 Python node. It is a full DDS participant — it subscribes and publishes like any other node — and it relays over a plain TCP socket. |
| **ROS-TCP-Connector** | Inside Unity | A Unity package: a TCP client, plus generated C# classes for ROS message types. |

Unity therefore never touches DDS. It talks to one ROS 2 node, over one socket, in a private
protocol. Five consequences follow, and each one is a thing that will confuse you at some point:

**The endpoint is a real node, so ordinary tools see it.** It appears in `ros2 node list`, and
`ros2 node info` shows every topic Unity asked it to subscribe to. When nothing moves in Unity, this
is the first place to look, and it splits the problem cleanly in two: if the endpoint is subscribed
and receiving, the fault is on the Unity side of the socket.

**Nothing happens until the endpoint is running.** Unity connects to a host and port; if there is no
listener, the scene runs perfectly and stays still. This is the single most common "it doesn't work"
and it produces no error in ROS 2 at all, because from ROS 2's point of view nothing was ever asked
for.

**QoS is the endpoint's, not Unity's.** The endpoint node makes the subscription, so the quality-of-
service profile in play is whatever it chose. Unity does not negotiate with the original publisher.
Topics that rely on non-default QoS — the latched, `transient_local` `robot_description` from
Chapter 1.4 is the obvious one — may behave differently than they do for a normal subscriber.

**It is a network link, which is a feature.** Unity and Gazebo both want the GPU, and on one laptop
they fight. Because the link is TCP, the Unity Editor can run on a different machine from ROS 2
entirely — a Windows workstation rendering a simulation that is running on a Linux box. You give the
connector that machine's address instead of `127.0.0.1`. For many readers this is the difference
between being able to follow this chapter and not.

**One socket means back-pressure.** Everything Unity subscribes to shares a connection. A scene
rendering at 12 fps will not consume a 100 Hz topic promptly, and the effect is delay, not a tidy
error. Publish to Unity at a rate a renderer can absorb — tens of hertz, not hundreds — and treat
the link as a view, never as a control path.

### Importing the humanoid

The robot in a Unity scene should come from `simple_humanoid.urdf` and nowhere else. Unity Robotics
provides a **URDF Importer** package that reads a URDF and builds the corresponding Unity hierarchy:
one GameObject per link, parented as the joint tree parents them, with each link's visual geometry
as a child mesh and an **`ArticulationBody`** component carrying the joint's type, axis, and limits.

Chapter 1.4's humanoid is an unusually easy import, and not by accident: every `<visual>` in it is a
`<box>`, `<cylinder>`, or `<sphere>`. There are no mesh files, so there are no missing assets, no
`package://` paths to resolve, and no scale surprises. A production URDF with STL or Collada meshes
needs those files reachable from the URDF's location before the import will succeed.

Two things about the imported hierarchy matter for everything that follows.

**Unity names GameObjects after links; URDF joints have their own names.** A `JointState` message
says `r_elbow_joint`, but the object in the Unity scene is called `r_lower_arm`, because that is the
child link the joint drives. The mapping — joint → its child link — is one you supply. It catches
everyone once.

**`ArticulationBody` drive targets are in degrees. ROS joint positions are in radians.** Send 1.2
radians to a drive expecting degrees and the arm moves 1.2 degrees; send a radian value where a
degree is wanted across the board and the robot moves 57.3 times too little, or too much if you get
the conversion backwards. This is the conversion referred to in the learning objectives, and it is
worth expecting before it happens.

There is a third conversion, which the importer handles for you but which you should know exists:
**ROS and Unity disagree about space.** ROS uses a right-handed, Z-up convention; Unity uses a
left-handed, Y-up one. Any *pose* crossing the boundary needs converting, and the connector package
ships helpers for exactly that. Joint *angles* are scalars about an axis the importer already set
up, which is one reason this chapter's example publishes joint angles rather than poses — it keeps
the example about the ROS 2 side, which is where the spec puts the focus.

## Running it

**None of the code or commands below has been executed by the author**, who wrote this chapter on
Windows with no ROS 2, no Gazebo, and no Unity install. The Python program was syntax-checked with
`python -m py_compile`; the ROS-side facts (package layout, launch file name, node name, parameter
names and defaults) were read from the `main-ros2` branch of the `Unity-Technologies/ROS-TCP-Endpoint`
repository. **Both examples are pending execution in a Unity-capable environment.** The C# script in
particular has never been compiled — it is included because a chapter about crossing a boundary that
shows only one side of it is not much use, and it is deliberately the smallest thing that can move a
robot.

A further honesty note, and it is the important one: **Unity Robotics' published ROS 2 support names
Foxy, not Jazzy.** The endpoint is a plain `ament_python` package whose runtime dependency is
`rclpy`, so building it on Jazzy is a normal source build rather than a port — but it is not a
combination the vendor tests, and you should expect to read build errors rather than assume none.
Nothing else in this book has that property; every other tool in Modules 1 and 2 is a supported
Jazzy pairing.

### Environment

The ROS 2 side is [Chapter 2.1](./gazebo-setup-and-basics.mdx)'s environment, unchanged: ROS 2
**Jazzy Jalisco** on **Ubuntu 24.04 (Noble)**, with Gazebo **Harmonic** if you want the humanoid
under physics as well. Source every terminal with `source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash`.

The Unity side is new, and it is heavy:

| What | Notes |
|---|---|
| Unity Hub + Unity Editor | Unity Robotics states 2020.2 or later; use a current LTS release. Several gigabytes, plus a Unity account and a licence tier you select at install. |
| ROS-TCP-Connector | Unity package, installed through the Editor's Package Manager from a git URL. |

Take the two Unity package git URLs from the `Unity-Technologies/Unity-Robotics-Hub` repository's own
instructions rather than from this page — they encode a repository subfolder and a release tag, and
they change between releases. Install them through **Window → Package Manager → Add package from git
URL**.

Build the endpoint once:

```bash
mkdir -p ~/unity_ws/src
cd ~/unity_ws/src
git clone -b main-ros2 https://github.com/Unity-Technologies/ROS-TCP-Endpoint.git
cd ~/unity_ws
colcon build
source ~/unity_ws/install/setup.bash
```

The `main-ros2` branch is the ROS 2 one; the default branch of that repository is for ROS 1 and will
not build here.

### Example 1: publishing joint angles from ROS 2

*Status: pending execution in a Unity-capable environment. Syntax-checked with `python -m
py_compile`; uses [Chapter 1.3](../ros2/python-agents-rclpy.mdx)'s node skeleton unchanged.*

This is the chapter's real example. It is an ordinary `rclpy` publisher — nothing in it knows Unity
exists, which is the point. It publishes `sensor_msgs/msg/JointState` on `/joint_states`, the same
message type and topic that [Chapter 1.4](../ros2/urdf-describing-a-humanoid.mdx)'s
`joint_state_publisher` GUI used to drive RViz. Anything that renders a robot from ROS 2 consumes
this topic, so a node that publishes it correctly can drive RViz, Unity, or both at once.

The motion is a wave: the right arm lifts, the elbow oscillates, the head turns to follow. Every
angle is clamped to the limits declared in `simple_humanoid.urdf`, because a renderer will happily
show you a joint bent past a limit that a physical robot would break on.

Save as `joint_wave.py`:

```python
"""Publishes a waving pose for simple_humanoid.urdf on /joint_states.

Nothing here is Unity-specific: /joint_states is the standard topic any
robot renderer consumes, so this drives RViz and a Unity scene equally.
"""

import math

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node

from sensor_msgs.msg import JointState

# The twelve revolute joints of simple_humanoid.urdf, in URDF order.
JOINT_NAMES = [
    'waist_joint',
    'neck_joint',
    'l_shoulder_joint',
    'l_elbow_joint',
    'r_shoulder_joint',
    'r_elbow_joint',
    'l_hip_joint',
    'l_knee_joint',
    'l_ankle_joint',
    'r_hip_joint',
    'r_knee_joint',
    'r_ankle_joint',
]

# lower, upper for the joints this node actually moves, copied from the
# <limit> elements of simple_humanoid.urdf. Publishing outside these is
# not an error -- a renderer will draw it -- so the node clamps instead.
LIMITS = {
    'neck_joint': (-1.57, 1.57),
    'r_shoulder_joint': (-2.00, 2.00),
    'r_elbow_joint': (-2.50, 0.00),
}

PUBLISH_HZ = 30.0    # a rate a renderer can absorb over one TCP socket
TOPIC = 'joint_states'


def clamp(name, value):
    low, high = LIMITS[name]
    return min(max(value, low), high)


class JointWave(Node):

    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('joint_wave')

        self.declare_parameter('wave_hz', 0.5)

        self.publisher = self.create_publisher(JointState, TOPIC, 10)
        self.create_timer(1.0 / PUBLISH_HZ, self.on_tick)
        self.create_timer(1.0, self.on_status)

        self.elapsed = 0.0
        self.angles = dict.fromkeys(JOINT_NAMES, 0.0)
        self.sent = 0

        self.get_logger().info('publishing %d joints on %s at %.0f Hz'
                               % (len(JOINT_NAMES), TOPIC, PUBLISH_HZ))

    def pose_at(self, t):
        """Joint angles, in radians, for time t seconds into the wave."""
        wave_hz = self.get_parameter('wave_hz').value
        phase = 2.0 * math.pi * wave_hz * t

        # Right arm raised and held; elbow swings through most of its
        # range; head turns slowly to look at the same side.
        self.angles['r_shoulder_joint'] = clamp('r_shoulder_joint', -1.40)
        self.angles['r_elbow_joint'] = clamp(
            'r_elbow_joint', -1.00 + 0.90 * math.sin(phase))
        self.angles['neck_joint'] = clamp(
            'neck_joint', -0.40 * math.sin(0.5 * phase))
        return self.angles

    def on_tick(self):
        self.elapsed += 1.0 / PUBLISH_HZ
        angles = self.pose_at(self.elapsed)

        msg = JointState()
        msg.header.stamp = self.get_clock().now().to_msg()
        msg.name = list(JOINT_NAMES)
        msg.position = [angles[name] for name in JOINT_NAMES]
        # velocity and effort stay empty: JointState allows it, and a
        # renderer only needs position.

        self.publisher.publish(msg)
        self.sent += 1

    def on_status(self):
        self.get_logger().info(
            'sent %d | elbow %+.3f rad (%+.1f deg) | neck %+.3f rad'
            % (self.sent,
               self.angles['r_elbow_joint'],
               math.degrees(self.angles['r_elbow_joint']),
               self.angles['neck_joint']))


def main(args=None):
    rclpy.init(args=args)
    node = JointWave()
    rclpy.spin(node)
    node.destroy_node()
    rclpy.shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

Check it works before Unity is anywhere in the picture. This is the same three-step discipline
Chapter 2.1 used for the Gazebo bridge — prove each hop separately, in order:

```bash
python3 joint_wave.py
```

```bash
ros2 topic echo /joint_states --field position
```

You should see a twelve-element list arriving 30 times a second, with the fifth element pinned at
`-1.4`, the sixth sweeping between `-1.9` and `-0.1`, and the second drifting slowly through
±`0.4`. The status line printed once a second gives you the same elbow angle in degrees, which is
the number to compare against Unity later.

If you want to see it on a robot immediately, and without installing anything: run Chapter 1.4's
`display_humanoid.launch.py` with its `joint_state_publisher` node removed, and this node drives
RViz directly. That is a useful sanity check and a fair reminder of the cheaper option.

### Example 2: the smallest Unity side that moves

*Status: **never compiled**. C#, and outside the environments available to the author. Included as
the minimum needed to see Example 1 work.*

Start the endpoint on the ROS 2 machine:

```bash
ros2 launch ros_tcp_endpoint endpoint.py
```

That starts a node called `UnityEndpoint`. It takes two parameters, `ROS_IP` (default `0.0.0.0`,
meaning it accepts connections on any interface) and `ROS_TCP_PORT` (default `10000`). Confirm it
came up:

```bash
ros2 node list
```

In Unity: import `simple_humanoid.urdf` with the URDF Importer, set the connector's ROS IP address
and port to match the endpoint (`127.0.0.1` and `10000` if both are on one machine), and attach the
script below to the imported robot's root object.

```csharp
using System.Collections.Generic;
using UnityEngine;
using Unity.Robotics.ROSTCPConnector;
using RosMessageTypes.Sensor;

// Drives three joints of the imported simple_humanoid from /joint_states.
public class JointStateReceiver : MonoBehaviour
{
    public string topicName = "/joint_states";

    // URDF names joints; the URDF Importer names GameObjects after links,
    // and puts the ArticulationBody on the joint's CHILD link. So the
    // mapping from a JointState name to a Unity object is joint -> child.
    static readonly Dictionary<string, string> JointToLink =
        new Dictionary<string, string>
        {
            { "neck_joint",       "head" },
            { "r_shoulder_joint", "r_upper_arm" },
            { "r_elbow_joint",    "r_lower_arm" }
        };

    readonly Dictionary<string, ArticulationBody> bodies =
        new Dictionary<string, ArticulationBody>();

    // Written by the message callback, read by Update. Targets are kept
    // in DEGREES, because that is what ArticulationDrive expects.
    readonly Dictionary<string, float> targetsDeg =
        new Dictionary<string, float>();

    void Start()
    {
        foreach (var body in GetComponentsInChildren<ArticulationBody>())
        {
            foreach (var pair in JointToLink)
            {
                if (body.name == pair.Value)
                {
                    bodies[pair.Key] = body;
                }
            }
        }
        Debug.Log("JointStateReceiver: matched " + bodies.Count + " of "
                  + JointToLink.Count + " joints");

        ROSConnection.GetOrCreateInstance()
                     .Subscribe<JointStateMsg>(topicName, OnJointState);
    }

    void OnJointState(JointStateMsg msg)
    {
        for (int i = 0; i < msg.name.Length && i < msg.position.Length; i++)
        {
            if (bodies.ContainsKey(msg.name[i]))
            {
                // ROS sends radians. Unity drives want degrees.
                targetsDeg[msg.name[i]] = (float)msg.position[i]
                                          * Mathf.Rad2Deg;
            }
        }
    }

    // Apply in Update so the scene is only ever touched from Unity's own
    // frame loop, whatever thread the message arrived on.
    void Update()
    {
        foreach (var pair in bodies)
        {
            float target;
            if (!targetsDeg.TryGetValue(pair.Key, out target))
            {
                continue;
            }
            var drive = pair.Value.xDrive;
            drive.target = target;
            pair.Value.xDrive = drive;
        }
    }
}
```

Press Play in Unity with `joint_wave.py` running, and the robot's right elbow should swing at the
rate the node's `wave_hz` parameter sets while the head turns at half that rate.

**When it does nothing, diagnose in this order** — the same order as the topic list:

1. `ros2 topic echo /joint_states` — is the data on the wire at all? If not, the fault is Example 1
   and Unity is innocent.
2. `ros2 node list` — is `UnityEndpoint` there? If not, nothing Unity does can work.
3. `ros2 node info /UnityEndpoint` — does it list `/joint_states` as a subscription? If not, Unity
   never connected, or connected to the wrong address.
4. The Unity console's `matched N of 3 joints` line. `matched 0 of 3` means the script is on the
   wrong object, or the imported link names differ from the ones in the table.
5. Only now suspect the drive: if the joint moves but in the wrong direction, that is a sign, and if
   it barely moves at all, that is the radians-and-degrees conversion.

That ordering is worth more than the script. Each step splits the system in half and rules out one
side, and the boundary this chapter is about is exactly where beginners lose an afternoon.

## Exercises

The first exercise needs only the ROS 2 environment. The rest need Unity as well.

**1. Prove the publisher without Unity.** Run `joint_wave.py`, then use `ros2 topic hz
/joint_states` and `ros2 topic echo /joint_states --field name` while changing `wave_hz` at runtime
with `ros2 param set /joint_wave wave_hz 2.0`.
*Correct result*: an average rate near 30 Hz that does **not** change when you change `wave_hz` —
`wave_hz` sets how fast the arm swings, not how often it is reported — and a twelve-element name
list in URDF order. Then set `wave_hz` to `0.0` and say what the elbow angle settles to and why it
is not zero.

**2. Break the clamp deliberately.** Change the elbow term from `-1.00 + 0.90 * math.sin(phase)` to
`-1.00 + 3.00 * math.sin(phase)` and watch the published value, then remove the `clamp` call for
that joint and watch it again.
*Correct result*: with the clamp, the value flattens at `-2.500` and `0.000` for part of each cycle
— the URDF's declared elbow limits — giving a visible pause at each end of the swing. Without it,
values of `+2.0` and `-4.0` are published and the renderer draws them. Say what this tells you about
where joint limits are enforced, and which of the tools in this module would have refused the same
angle.

**3. Make the joint-to-link mapping fail on purpose.** Change `"r_elbow_joint"` in the C# table to
map to `"r_upper_arm"` instead of `"r_lower_arm"`, and predict what you will see before you press
Play.
*Correct result*: `matched 3 of 3` — nothing reports an error, because both link names exist — and
the shoulder now receives the elbow's angle while the elbow receives nothing and holds still. Two
joints wrong from one edit. Say why this class of mistake produces no diagnostic anywhere in the
system, and what you would add to `Start()` to catch it.

**4. Decide, in writing, for four tasks.** For each of the following, say Unity or Gazebo or RViz,
and give the one-sentence reason: (a) checking whether the humanoid's arm reaches into a doorway in
a modelled office; (b) checking whether the humanoid tips over when its right foot's friction is
halved; (c) checking that the depth camera from Chapter 2.3 is mounted facing forward and not
backward; (d) producing a thirty-second clip for a funding review.
*Correct result*: (a) Unity — the environment and how it reads to a human are the question;
(b) Gazebo — it is a physics question and Gazebo is the authority; (c) RViz — it is a frame
question, it is already installed, and Chapter 1.4 showed you how; (d) Unity — the pixels are
literally the product. If you answered Unity to (b), reread "What Unity does not replace".

That closes the digital twin. The humanoid now has a body that physics tests, sensors that report
what a real robot's would report, and — when it is worth the toolchain — a view a person outside the
project can understand.

What it still does not have is any opinion of its own. Nothing in this module decides anything: the
fall in Chapter 2.2 was gravity, the readings in Chapter 2.3 were measurements, and the wave in this
chapter was a sine function you wrote. Turning sensor data into an estimate of where the robot is,
and that estimate into a decision about where to go, is the next module's subject.
