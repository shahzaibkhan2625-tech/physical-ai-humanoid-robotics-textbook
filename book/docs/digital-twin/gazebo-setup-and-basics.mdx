# Gazebo Setup & Simulation Basics

[Chapter 1.4](../ros2/urdf-describing-a-humanoid.mdx) produced a robot that has never been wrong.
`simple_humanoid.urdf` asserts that the pelvis weighs 4 kg and that the left knee bends 2.2 radians
and no further, and nothing has tested either claim. `check_urdf` will tell you the file is a valid
tree; no tool in Module 1 will tell you the robot falls over.

This chapter hands the file to a physics engine, and on the way has to solve a problem that has
nothing to do with physics: Gazebo and ROS 2 are separate programs with separate messaging systems,
and moving a number from one to the other takes a third process whose existence surprises most
people the first time.

## Learning objectives

By the end of this chapter you will be able to:

- Launch a Gazebo Harmonic simulation containing this book's humanoid, spawned into an empty world.
- Verify from a Python ROS 2 client that the simulator and ROS 2 are actually exchanging data.
- Explain why a bridge process is needed at all, and use that to diagnose a Gazebo topic that never
  appears on the ROS 2 side.

## What a simulator is for

Three things, worth separating because they fail differently.

**You can be wrong cheaply.** A control bug on real hardware bends a leg; the same bug in simulation
costs a restart. [Chapter 1.1](../ros2/physical-ai-embodied-intelligence.mdx) made the point that
physical actions are irreversible, which is what makes iteration expensive. A simulator suspends that
property on purpose.

**You do not have to wait for hardware.** Most of this book's robot does not exist. Perception,
navigation, and language planning can be written and debugged against a simulated body years before
there is a physical one.

**Initial conditions are repeatable.** The underrated one. Put a real robot back "where it was" and
it is within a centimetre or two, on a floor whose friction changed when someone mopped it. Reset a
simulation and every mass, pose, and contact parameter is what it was last time — so a test that
answers differently on the second run is telling you about your code, not the room.

The cost is that **the simulator is a model, not the world**: contact is approximated, friction is a
number someone chose, and your collision geometry is a sphere standing in for a head. The distance
between "it worked in simulation" and "it worked" is the **sim-to-real gap**, introduced in
[Chapter 1.1](../ros2/physical-ai-embodied-intelligence.mdx) and not repeated here.

## Which Gazebo

**Gazebo Harmonic** is the simulator and **ROS 2 Jazzy Jalisco** is the middleware. They are separate
projects from separate release cycles, and neither contains the other; a third project, **`ros_gz`**,
connects them. The pairing is deliberate — Harmonic is the officially supported Gazebo for Jazzy,
both are long-term-support releases, and both are supported to **May 2029**, so they reach end of
life together.

One warning before you search for anything. There are two generations of Gazebo, and the internet is
full of the older one.

| | Current | Previous generation |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Gazebo (Harmonic, Ionic, …) | Gazebo Classic |
| Command | `gz sim` | `gazebo`, `gzserver`, `gzclient` |
| ROS integration | `ros_gz` | `gazebo_ros` |

Gazebo Classic reached end of life in January 2025. A tutorial that tells you to run `gzserver` or
install `gazebo-ros-pkgs` does not apply here.

## Worlds, models, and spawning

Three words, used precisely.

A **world** is the environment: physics settings, lighting, ground, and the simulation systems that
are loaded. It is described in **SDF** — Simulation Description Format, an XML format related to
URDF but larger, because URDF describes one robot while SDF describes a whole scene. A world has a
name, and that name matters at the command line: the world inside Gazebo's shipped `empty.sdf` is
called `empty`.

A **model** is a thing placed in a world — a robot, a table, a ball — with links and joints exactly
as in URDF.

**Spawning** is inserting a model into a world that is *already running*, which is not the same as
listing it in the world file. You start an empty world and then, seconds later, ask the running
simulator to add a robot. It is why you never edit a world file to try a different robot.

Underneath, a world's contents form a **scene graph**: a tree of entities, each positioned relative
to its parent, with the world at the root. Links hang off their model, the model off the world —
[Chapter 1.4](../ros2/urdf-describing-a-humanoid.mdx)'s parent-child tree, one level up. And "empty"
is a small lie: `empty.sdf` holds a directional light called `sun`, a static `ground_plane` 100 m
square, a physics block, and four systems. Remove the ground plane and your robot falls forever.

## One step at a time

A physics engine does not run continuously. It **steps**: take the state of every body, compute the
forces acting over one small slice of time, integrate to get new positions and velocities, resolve
the contacts that produced, stop. Then do it again. `empty.sdf` sets `max_step_size` to `0.001`, so
one step is one millisecond of simulated time.

That gives you two clocks, and confusing them causes a whole class of bugs. **Simulation time** is
the sum of the steps taken: after a thousand 1 ms steps it is 1.000 s, regardless of how long that
took. **Wall-clock time** is the time on your watch. Their ratio is the **real-time factor** (RTF),
which Gazebo shows at the bottom of its window and publishes on a statistics topic. RTF 1.0 means the
simulation keeps pace with reality; below 1.0 means the machine cannot compute steps fast enough — a
heavy scene, an expensive collision mesh, a step size you shrank. Above 1.0 happens on trivial
scenes, which is why "wait 5 seconds" is never a valid way to time anything in simulation.

The consequence for your code is concrete. Every ROS 2 node has a boolean parameter
**`use_sim_time`**, which you saw unexplained in the parameter listing in
[Chapter 1.3](../ros2/python-agents-rclpy.mdx). Set it `true` and the node stops reading the system
clock: `self.get_clock().now()` returns whatever last arrived on the ROS 2 `/clock` topic. Every node
touching simulator data needs it, or timestamps from different nodes describe different timelines —
one stamping a reading at simulated t = 3.2 s, another stamping its response at wall-clock
1786000000 s. Nothing errors; the data is just nonsense.

## Two messaging systems, one robot

Here is the part that costs people an afternoon.

ROS 2 nodes talk over DDS, as [Chapter 1.2](../ros2/ros2-architecture.mdx) describes, exchanging
types like `sensor_msgs/msg/Imu`. Gazebo does not use DDS. It has its own publish–subscribe library,
**Gazebo Transport**, carrying its own types — `gz.msgs.Clock`, `gz.msgs.Pose_V` — defined in
Protocol Buffers rather than in ROS `.msg` files.

Two transports, two type systems, no overlap. A Gazebo topic is **invisible** to `ros2 topic list`,
and a ROS 2 topic is invisible to `gz topic -l`. These are not one system with two front ends. They
are two systems that happen to share a machine.

So `ros_gz_bridge` exists: an ordinary process that joins both networks, subscribes to a named topic
on one side, converts each message to the equivalent type on the other, and republishes it. You tell
it topic by topic which pairs to translate and in which direction. It translates only what you ask
for; there is no automatic mode.

That one fact settles most "my topic isn't appearing" problems, and makes the diagnosis mechanical:

1. **Gazebo is not producing it.** Check `gz topic -l`. If it is absent there, the fault is in the
   model or the world, not the bridge.
2. **The bridge was not told about it.** Present in `gz topic -l`, absent from `ros2 topic list`.
3. **The types do not match.** The bridge runs but nothing arrives; compare your pair against the
   table in the
   [`ros_gz_bridge` documentation](https://github.com/gazebosim/ros_gz/blob/jazzy/ros_gz_bridge/README.md).

A reader who does not know the bridge exists will look for all three in the wrong place.

## Spawning the humanoid

The robot here is the robot from [Chapter 1.4](../ros2/urdf-describing-a-humanoid.mdx), unchanged:
`simple_humanoid.urdf`, root link `base_link`, thirteen links and twelve revolute joints. Gazebo
reads SDF and this is URDF, but that is handled — `libsdformat`, the library Gazebo parses scene
files with, contains a URDF parser and converts the file internally when it sees a `<robot>` root
element. Two honest limits, both better known in advance.

**Height.** With every joint at zero the soles sit 0.85 m below `base_link`, and `base_link` is the
spawned model's origin. Spawn at z = 0 and the legs are buried in the ground plane; spawn at z = 0.9
and the soles start 5 cm above it.

**No joint states.** The Chapter 1.4 URDF carries no `<gazebo>` tags, so no Gazebo systems attach to
the model. Spawned, it is a real physical object — it has mass, it collides, it falls — and it
publishes nothing about its joints. The world's scene broadcaster publishes link *poses* on
`/world/empty/pose/info`, so you can see where the parts are, but no `sensor_msgs/msg/JointState`
exists: producing one is a system plugin's job, and this model loads none. That is a limit of the
model as written, and this chapter does not fix it.

## Running it

**None of the commands or programs below have been executed by the author**, who wrote this chapter
on Windows with no ROS 2, no Gazebo, and no OpenGL-capable Linux graphics stack. Every command, flag,
package name, and message type was verified against the official Gazebo Harmonic documentation and
the `jazzy` branches of the `ros_gz`, `gz-sim`, `gz-transport`, and `rclpy` sources, and the Python
program was syntax-checked, but **all three examples are pending execution in a Gazebo-capable
environment**. Where output is described it comes from the source that produces it, not a transcript.

### Environment

All three examples assume ROS 2 **Jazzy Jalisco** and Gazebo **Harmonic** on **Ubuntu 24.04
(Noble)**, with a working display and OpenGL drivers for the GUI steps.

```bash
sudo apt update
sudo apt install ros-jazzy-ros-gz
```

`ros-jazzy-ros-gz` is a meta-package: it pulls in `ros_gz_sim` (the `create` spawner), `ros_gz_bridge`
(the translator), `ros_gz_image`, and — because as of Jazzy the ROS repository ships Gazebo itself as
vendor packages — Gazebo Harmonic and the `gz` command-line tool. Do not also add the
`packages.osrfoundation.org` repository; mixing the two sources causes version conflicts that are
tedious to unpick.

Source every terminal below with `source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash`, and put `simple_humanoid.urdf`
somewhere you can name absolutely — the commands assume `~/humanoid/simple_humanoid.urdf`.

### Example 1: a world, and a robot in it

*Status: pending execution in a Gazebo-capable environment.*

First terminal — start the simulator on Gazebo's shipped empty world:

```bash
gz sim -v 4 empty.sdf
```

`-v 4` is maximum console verbosity, which is what you want the first time; the default is `1`. The
GUI opens on a grey ground plane, and **the simulation starts paused**. A `-r` flag runs it on start;
this example skips it deliberately, because a paused world is one you can spawn into and look at
before anything moves.

Second terminal — the running world exposes a service, `/world/empty/create`, for inserting entities;
`gz service -l | grep create` will show it. Rather than call it directly, use the `create` executable
that `ros_gz_sim` ships, which builds the same request from the ROS 2 side:

```bash
ros2 run ros_gz_sim create \
  -world empty \
  -file ~/humanoid/simple_humanoid.urdf \
  -name simple_humanoid \
  -z 0.9
```

`-world` is the world to insert into — `empty` is the `<world>` element's name inside `empty.sdf` —
and omitting it makes `create` ask the server for its worlds and take the first. `-file` is the model
path, converted from URDF on the way in; `-name` is the entity's name in the scene graph; `-z` is the
initial height, putting the soles 5 cm clear of the ground. `create` runs, prints, and exits; on
success its last line is:

```text
[INFO] [...] [ros_gz_sim]: Entity creation successful.
```

The humanoid is now standing in the GUI, grey and blue with orange feet — and standing only because
the world is paused.

Press **Play**. It drops 5 cm, its feet hit the plane, and it collapses: nothing holds a joint at an
angle and nothing is balancing it. That is not a mistake in what you just did, and it is the subject
of the next chapter in this module. Leave the simulation running.

### Example 2: what Gazebo is saying, and what ROS 2 hears

*Status: pending execution in a Gazebo-capable environment.*

Back in the second terminal, list what the simulator is publishing:

```bash
gz topic -l
gz topic -e -t /clock -n 1
```

The listing includes `/clock` and `/world/empty/clock`, `/stats` and `/world/empty/stats`, and
`/world/empty/pose/info` — the world-namespaced topics, and a copy at the root where nothing else has
claimed the name. `/clock` carries `gz.msgs.Clock` and `/stats` the `gz.msgs.WorldStatistics` that
holds the real-time factor. The second command echoes one `/clock` message and stops.

Now the point. Third terminal:

```bash
source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash
ros2 topic list
```

`/clock` is not there, and neither is anything else from Gazebo — only `/parameter_events` and
`/rosout`, which always are. Nothing is broken; nothing has been asked to translate. So ask, and
leave it running:

```bash
ros2 run ros_gz_bridge parameter_bridge /clock@rosgraph_msgs/msg/Clock[gz.msgs.Clock
```

That argument is one string with a grammar: topic name, `@`, ROS 2 message type, a **direction
symbol**, Gazebo type.

| Symbol | Direction |
|---|---|
| `@` | bidirectional |
| `[` | Gazebo → ROS 2 |
| `]` | ROS 2 → Gazebo |

The direction is not decoration, and the `ros_gz_bridge` documentation calls this case out: a
bidirectional `/clock` bridge would make ROS 2 a publisher on Gazebo's `/clock`, and Gazebo, finding
another publisher there, stops publishing the root topic and uses only `/world/empty/clock`. One
character decides whether the clock works.

Fourth terminal:

```bash
source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash
ros2 topic info /clock
```

`/clock` is now a ROS 2 topic of type `rosgraph_msgs/msg/Clock` with one publisher — the bridge — and
`ros2 topic echo /clock` prints simulated seconds ticking upward. Two messaging systems, one process
joining them, nothing else changed.

### Example 3: a Python client that proves the link

*Status: pending execution in a Gazebo-capable environment. Syntax-checked with `python -m
py_compile`; follows [Chapter 1.3](../ros2/python-agents-rclpy.mdx)'s node skeleton exactly.*

`ros2 topic echo` proves the bridge is up; it does not prove that *your* code can use what crosses
it. This node subscribes to the bridged `/clock`, measures how fast simulated time advances against a
Python wall-clock reading, and reports its own ROS clock alongside. `rosgraph_msgs` ships with any
ROS 2 installation, so there is nothing extra to install. Save this as `sim_link_check.py`:

```python
"""Reports whether Gazebo and ROS 2 are exchanging data over the /clock bridge."""

import time

import rclpy
from rclpy.node import Node

from rosgraph_msgs.msg import Clock

REPORT_PERIOD = 2.0   # s of wall time between report lines


def stamp_seconds(stamp):
    """Convert a builtin_interfaces/msg/Time into float seconds."""
    return stamp.sec + stamp.nanosec * 1e-9


class SimLinkCheck(Node):

    def __init__(self):
        super().__init__('sim_link_check')

        self.clock_sub = self.create_subscription(
            Clock, '/clock', self.on_clock, 10)
        self.report_timer = self.create_timer(REPORT_PERIOD, self.on_report)

        self.messages = 0
        self.first_sim = None
        self.first_wall = None
        self.latest_sim = 0.0

        self.get_logger().info('waiting for /clock from the simulator')

    def on_clock(self, msg):
        self.latest_sim = stamp_seconds(msg.clock)
        self.messages += 1
        if self.first_sim is None:
            self.first_sim = self.latest_sim
            self.first_wall = time.monotonic()
            self.get_logger().info(
                'bridge is alive: first /clock message at sim time %.3f s'
                % self.first_sim)

    def on_report(self):
        if self.first_sim is None:
            self.get_logger().warning(
                'no /clock messages yet: is gz sim running, and is the '
                'bridge started?')
            return

        sim_elapsed = self.latest_sim - self.first_sim
        wall_elapsed = time.monotonic() - self.first_wall
        if wall_elapsed <= 0.0:
            return

        node_clock = self.get_clock().now().nanoseconds * 1e-9

        self.get_logger().info(
            '%d msgs | sim +%.2f s over %.2f s wall | rtf %.2f | node clock %.3f s'
            % (self.messages, sim_elapsed, wall_elapsed,
               sim_elapsed / wall_elapsed, node_clock))


def main(args=None):
    rclpy.init(args=args)
    node = SimLinkCheck()
    rclpy.spin(node)
    node.destroy_node()
    rclpy.shutdown()


if __name__ == '__main__':
    main()
```

Nothing is new except the message type. Two details are worth naming: `time.monotonic()` is plain
Python and unaffected by `use_sim_time`, which is exactly why it is the right thing to measure
simulated time *against*; and `self.get_clock().now()` is the node's ROS clock, the value that
changes meaning depending on that parameter.

Run it in a fifth terminal, with `gz sim` and the bridge still up:

```bash
source /opt/ros/jazzy/setup.bash
python3 sim_link_check.py --ros-args -p use_sim_time:=true
```

`use_sim_time` needs no `declare_parameter` call: every `rclpy` node declares it during construction,
which is why it appeared in `ros2 param list` in Chapter 1.3. The node logs `waiting for /clock from
the simulator`, then one `bridge is alive` line, then a report every two seconds. Three fields to
read:

- **`msgs`** rising confirms messages are crossing the bridge and reaching your callback.
- **`rtf`** is simulated seconds per wall second, computed by your own code, and should sit near the
  real-time factor at the bottom of the Gazebo window: the same quantity, measured by two
  independent observers, and their agreement is the strongest evidence you will get that the two
  systems are genuinely connected.
- **`node clock`** should track the simulator's clock. A number in the billions is seconds since
  1970, meaning `use_sim_time` did not take effect.

If you get the `no /clock messages yet` warning instead, work down the three-step diagnosis above.
Stop the node, the bridge, and Gazebo with `Ctrl+C`.

## Exercises

Each needs the environment above, `simple_humanoid.urdf`, and `sim_link_check.py`.

**1. Freeze one clock and not the other.** With everything running, press the GUI's Pause button,
watch three report lines, then press Play.
*Correct result*: `msgs` keeps climbing while paused — Gazebo publishes `/clock` on every pass of its
loop whether or not it is stepping — but `sim` stops growing, `rtf` falls toward `0.00`, and
`node clock` stops moving; on Play, `rtf` climbs back. Say in one sentence why a node with
`use_sim_time: true` and a one-second timer would stop firing during the pause.

**2. Take the parameter away.** Restart the node with no `--ros-args` at all, then again with
`-p use_sim_time:=false`.
*Correct result*: identical both times, because `false` is the default. `msgs` and `rtf` are
unchanged, but `node clock` now reads a number above 1,700,000,000 — seconds since the Unix epoch —
instead of the simulator's few seconds. State which reading a timestamp on simulated sensor data has
to agree with, and what breaks if half your nodes use each.

**3. Cut the bridge, keep the simulator.** Leave `gz sim` and the node running and stop only the
`parameter_bridge`. Run `gz topic -l | grep clock` and `ros2 topic info /clock`, then restart it.
*Correct result*: Gazebo still lists `/clock` and `/world/empty/clock` — it never stopped publishing
— while `ros2 topic info /clock` reports `Publisher count: 0` with the subscription still there, and
the node starts warning. Restarting the bridge resumes reports with nothing else restarted. Name
which of the three diagnostic steps this is, and what `ros2 topic list` alone would have told you.

**4. Bridge the humanoid's poses.** Find the Gazebo type on `/world/empty/pose/info` with
`gz topic -i -t /world/empty/pose/info`, look it up in the
[`ros_gz_bridge` mapping table](https://github.com/gazebosim/ros_gz/blob/jazzy/ros_gz_bridge/README.md),
and bridge it Gazebo → ROS 2 only.
*Correct result*: `gz.msgs.Pose_V` pairs with `tf2_msgs/msg/TFMessage`, giving the argument
`/world/empty/pose/info@tf2_msgs/msg/TFMessage[gz.msgs.Pose_V`, after which `ros2 topic info` reports
one publisher and `ros2 topic echo` shows one transform per link. Say why this is *not* a substitute
for the joint states the model does not publish.

You now have the robot in a world and a Python client that can see it. What you do not have is any
idea why it fell down. That is next: what the physics engine computes on each of those
one-millisecond steps, and which numbers in `simple_humanoid.urdf` it takes far more literally than
you meant them.
